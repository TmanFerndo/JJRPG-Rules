# Overview
This system is designed to be quick and easy to use, while also maintaining some level of consistency and fairness. To accomplish this, many aspects of this system have been relegated to dice rolls. This allows for arbitrary decisions to be made without much bias. In addition, this also streamlines the process for determining damage and other outcomes for the sake of speed. This system assumes you already have base level knowledge and experience with TTRPG systems like Dungeons and Dragons and Pathfinder. If you do not, then it is recommended you learn some of the basics of those systems or seek help from your GM. It is certainly not recommended you run a game with this system as your first TTRPG.

# Ability Checks and Saving Throws
Sometimes whether or not your sorcerer is capable enough to successfully complete an action is not clear based on stats alone. Maybe you are not fast enough to dodge out of the way of an attack, maybe you are not strong enough to move a large piece of debris. In these cases, you will be called to make an Ability Check. Ability Checks are determined by rolling a ten sided dice (d10) and adding the relevant Stat. For example, a Strength Check would have you roll a d10 and add your Strength Score to the roll.
### Difficulty Class
The Difficulty Class of an Ability Check is what determines how difficult it is to succeed. In order to pass an Ability Check your final total after adding any relevant bonuses must be greater than or equal to the Difficulty Class. Difficulty Classes are often written as shown: DC 6. This means that the number you need to meet is 6. Difficulty Classes are arranged into relative difficulties as shown below:
- Flyhead (Easy): DC 3
- Grade 4 (Medium): DC 6
- Grade 3 (Hard): DC 9
- Grade 2 (Very Hard): DC 12
- Grade 1 (Extreme): DC 15
- Special Grade: DC 18
- Gojo level: DC 21
- Impossible: DC 24
- Divine: DC 27

There are various types of Ability Checks that will crop up through these rules. The three most common types are Contested Checks, Attack Rolls, and Saving Throws.
### Contested Checks
When two or more sorcerers are competing against each other, they may need to make an ability check to determine success. Instead of rolling against a Difficulty Class, sorcerers will roll a d10 and add the relevant stat. Whoever has the higher value wins.
### Attack Rolls
Attack Rolls can be thought of as Perception Checks. In order to hit an opponent and deal damage, you must make an attack roll. Roll a d10 and add your Perception score. Compare the result to your opponent's Speed Class. Speed Class functions as the DC for the Attack Roll. Attack Rolls and Speed Class are explained in more detail later.
### Saving Throws
When you are caught in an Area of Effect, Technique Effect, or environmental conditions, you may need to make an ability check to succeed. This is called a Saving Throw. In most cases, effects that require a Saving Throw have a predetermined DC based on how hard it is to succeed. For example, a large explosion may have a DC 15 Speed Saving Throw required to avoid damage.
# Accuracy and Difficulty
In Jujutsu RPG players will encounter scenarios that have varying difficulties. To represent the changes in environment—and the relative ease of some activities—we use Accuracy and Difficulty.

**Accuracy** is used to denote situations where a character is more likely to succeed.
- ex. **+1 Accuracy** to attack rolls

**Difficulty** is used to denote situations where a character is less likely to succeed.
- ex. **+1 Difficulty** to saving throws

When a character receives either Accuracy or Difficulty, they roll a d4. Depending on if they have Accuracy or Difficulty, they will either add or subtract the result from the relevant ability check, attack roll, or saving throw respectively.

When a player has both Accuracy and Difficulty, you subtract the larger amount from the smaller and use whatever is left over.
- ex. if player A has 3 points of Accuracy and 2 points of Difficulty, then they would subtract the two points of Difficulty from their 3 points of Accuracy. This would leave them with only 1 point of Accuracy.

For both Accuracy and Difficulty, having a final total greater than one means you roll multiple d4's and choose the highest value.
- ex. if Player B has 3 points of Accuracy and 0 points of Difficulty, they would roll 3d4 and choose the highest value. They then add the result to the relevant roll.
  
**Accuracy** always adds to a roll and **Difficulty** always subtracts from a roll, however you always choose the highest d4 value when rolling multiple points of either **Accuracy** or **Difficulty**.
# The Crucible of Fate
In Jujutsu RPG—and within the larger setting of Jujutsu Kaisen as whole—Fate is a very real concept. Not necessarily to the extent that all events are predetermined, but there is an apparent "flow" to things. Cursed Energy itself seems to have some sort of "will" that links sorcerers, curses, and places across time and space. Sometimes, the "flow" of Cursed Energy coalesces in just the right way to sway events for or against individuals.

In Jujutsu RPG the "flow" of causality is represented by the Crucible of Fate.
### Fate Dice
At the beginning of each session the GM and every player will roll 1d4. These are "Fate Dice" and are used to represent the tides of Fate. After everyone has rolled their d4 they will reveal the result:
- on a 1-2 the die goes to the GM's side of the Crucible
- on a 3-4 the die goes to the player's side of the Crucible

Fate Dice last until the end of the session and can be spent to cause various effects to occur. These effects often bend the standard interpretation of the rules in the favor of the person who spent the Fate Die. When a Fate Die is expended it does not go away. Instead it transfers to the other side of the Crucible.

Ex. If the GM spends 1 Fate Die it goes to the players side. Vice versa, if a player spends 1 Fate Die it goes to the GM's side.

**There is no "double dipping" with Fate Dice. If a Fate Die is expended by the GM or by the players, it cannot be used by either until the current Fate Dice's effects have resolved.**

# Round Down Rule
If a roll, buff, debuff or other effect causes your stats or rolls to be a fractional number, always round down unless otherwise stated.
# Specific Over General
If there is ever a rule that seems to contradict a previously stated rule, the more specific rule overrides the general rule. For example, if a Cursed Technique allows you to boost a Stat above 6, then the Techniques rule takes precedence.
# Missions
In JJRPG, play is structured around missions. As Jujutsu Sorcerers, the player's job is to deal with the supernatural threats of curses and curse users. By going on missions, the players can accomplish this task as well as achieve larger goals.

Missions will always have clearly stated goals and stakes that are established before embarking on said mission. Regardless of what, there's always something that needs doing.

Each mission will start with these steps:
- Briefing: The mission goal is established by either the players or GM. Then the GM sets the stakes and/or consequences.
- Preparation: the players spend resources like money and MP to gather tools and allies for the mission.
- Reserves: the players establish what reserves they are taking on the mission
- Mission Start: the players travel to the mission location and arrive at the scene.

At the end of a mission—regardless of success or failure—players will gain MP. Though players will likely gain more MP for successful missions, they can still learn from failure. After MP has been dolled out and the players have had a chance to roleplay any post-mission conversations, play proceeds to downtime.
### Step 1: Briefing
Briefing is the beginning of every mission. Whether being informed by a mentor, reading it from a mission board, or discussing it with their fellow sorcerers, this is where the players figure out what needs to be done.

This scene doesn't necessarily need to be an in-character conversation, but it does need to establish three things: The situation, goal, and stakes.

**The Situation** - The GM will provide the players with whatever information about the current situation (before the players involvement) as they deem necessary. This can be as short or as long as is needed but should clearly establish what has happened/is happening at the start of the mission.

**The Goal** - Regardless of the situation, the Goal is what the players themselves want to accomplish. Depending on the situation this goal might align with the same objectives that the GM outlined, but they might also be completely different.

If the players receive orders from the Jujutsu HQ Hire-Ups that conflict with their personal beliefs, perhaps the players decide to go rogue. Or, perhaps intentionally failing a mission furthers the players goals somehow.

The players can also set a goal on their own without input from the GM. Perhaps they decide to pursue a villain who got away, or investigate a hideout they think might be connected to a larger plot.

Regardless, success or failure depends on the completion of the players goals not the ones set out by the GM, though the two often align.

A mission's goals can also change during the mission. Perhaps some new threat or condition pops up that alters the conditions of success. Or perhaps some new piece of information comes to light that changes the perspective of the players.

**The Stakes** - Once a goal has been established, it's time to set the stakes. The stakes are the outcomes, significance, and consequences to the players and world due to the actions of the players within a mission.

Stakes are typically determined by the GM but they are often related to or influenced by the actions, history, backgrounds, and desires of the players. A player who has ties to the clan they have been tasked with assisting might have a deeper personal stake in the situation for example.

The stakes are what the players—or whoever they are working for—stand to lose and/or gain. If the players are successful, they may stand to gain reputation, money, and resources. If they fail, then they could experience bodily harm, loss of reputation, or even death in extreme cases.
### Step 2: Preparation
Once the Briefing is complete, the players prepare their sorcerers for the mission. This may include purchasing any items or Cursed Tools, as well as spending any MP if they have the time.

Generally speaking, players will have the opportunity to decide what their sorcerer is bringing with them on the mission. Players can take up to two Weapons and up to five Cursed Tools/utility items.
- Weapons that are Cursed Tools cannot be brought along as utility items. They count as Weapons for the purpose of assigning gear.
### Step 3: Reserves
The last thing to do before starting a mission is to establish what reserves the players are bringing into the field. Reserves is a catch-all term for extra gear, support, reinforcements, information, and anything else that the sorcerers may have access to during a mission. Reserves are often acquired during downtime but they can also be obtained during a mission.

There is no specific definition for what counts as Reserves. As long as the GM agrees you can bring it with you, it counts as Reserves.
### Step 4: Mission Start
Once all of the preliminary set up is finished, play moves directly to Mission Start. This is where the game cuts straight to when the players arrive at the mission scene. This doesn't necessarily mean starting combat, but is more generally when the “action” begins.

During Mission Start, the GM will describe the scene and then turn things over to the players to respond. Perhaps they need to investigate some ruins or a haunted sight. Maybe they are going into an underground Cursed Tool market to track down a known criminal. Whatever mission parameters have been set, this is when the scene begins.
### Step 5: Debriefing
After the mission is completed (successfully or otherwise), the characters have a moment to sit down and take stock of the situation. This is the Debrief. While it doesn't have to be an in character conversation, much like Briefing it is encouraged.

There are a few things that need to happen in the Debrief:
1. The GM gives out rewards. This can include money, MP, Cursed Tools, or other resources.
2. Talk about the mission. While not strictly necessary, it can be a useful step that allows the players (and the characters) to take stock of the situation and potentially progress the story. If there were any stand out moments or tense situations, ask how your character is reacting to that situation. Were there any wake-up calls? Or did the mission reinforce any previously held beliefs? Just remember that roleplay is meant to be collaborative, not competitive.
3. The GM explains some general consequences. While not all consequences of a mission will be immediately understood by the players, the GM should take some time to concretely establish the direct consequences of success or failure. Maybe the villain got away, or perhaps the city was saved.
# Resting
As your Sorcerer goes throughout their journey, they will expend resources like Cursed Energy, and Health Points. These resources are limited and regenerate over a long period of time. In order to regenerate spent resources, your Sorcerer can’t just go to sleep or eat food like normal. They need to spend dedicated time to recover. This is called Resting. Rests are divided into two main categories: Long Rests and Short Rests.
### Long Rests
A Long Rest is a rest that takes up three days. Your Sorcerer has access to one Long Rest a week and they regain their expended Long Rest at the start of every week (midnight on Sunday). When you take a Long Rest, you cannot participate in any Downtime Activities for the duration of the rest. Upon the completion of a Long Rest, a sorcerer regains 50% of their Maximum CE and 25% of their Maximum HP. In addition, a sorcerer heals from up to 6 Minor Wounds.
### Short Rests
A Short Rest is a rest that takes up only one day. Your Sorcerer has access to two Short Rests a week, however they can regain one expended Short Rest at the end of a Long Rest. Expended Short Rests are regained at the start of every week (midnight on Sunday). When you take a Short Rest, you cannot participate in any Downtime Activities for the duration of the rest. Upon the completion of a Short Rest, a Sorcerer regains 30% of their Maximum CE and 15% of their Maximum HP. In addition, a sorcerer heals from 3 Minor Wounds.
# Time
Time passes at different intervals depending on what’s happening. During Combat, turns pass at a rate of a few seconds per turn and most Combat exchanges don’t take longer than a few minutes. While each turn probably won’t take longer than one to two seconds, the GM can make a few arbitrary decisions as to exactly how much time passes during Combat. Outside of Combat, things progress at a much more gentle pace. Conversations between players and NPC’s progress in real time while travel and Downtime progress at arbitrary intervals of hours and days.
# Downtime
Sorcerers are people. They are individuals with extraordinary abilities and power, but they still have lives. For young Jujutsu students this life can become quite cramped between missions and school, but they still get to take some time off to pursue their own interests. It is always assumed that the player characters have something interesting to do between missions. This is represented as Downtime.

The amount of downtime the characters have access to can vary drastically depending on the story. Perhaps a nefarious curse user has started a nation wide competition between sorcerers that demands participation with the consequence of refusal being death. In that situation, downtime would likely be limited and short, but still present to a degree. In other scenarios, sorcerers could potentially go weeks or even months between notable missions, thus having way more time to pursue their own interests.

There will always be some downtime between missions even if it's only a few hours. This gives the players time to prepare for the next mission and take steps to progress the direction of the story.
### Structured and Freeform Play
There are two components to downtime: structured downtime actions, and freeform play. Downtime Actions are defined activities that allow players to accumulate reserves for the next mission. Each player gets a limited number of these actions – usually one, but sometimes two for especially long periods of downtime. If the players are under siege, for example, they probably only have time for a single action. The kinds of actions that characters can take are outlined below. 

However, the purpose of downtime isn’t just to prepare for the next mission – it’s also for players to tell personal stories, advance plots, and flesh out characters. Players can do as much freeform roleplaying as they like during downtime – maybe making skill checks, maybe just talking and interacting. Even if their actions are totally unrelated to preparing for a mission, the GM can still feel free to reward this kind of play in ways that will help them in the field. 

For example, even if Geto and Gojo simply hang out together with no intent to prepare something, gain reserves, or take downtime actions, the GM might decide that their new bond grants them +1 accuracy on skill checks to help each other during the next mission.
### Reserves
Downtime can be used to prepare reserves for the next mission. Reserves covers anything being held as an advantage for the next mission, including extra supplies, gear, support, bonuses, allies, and so on. The GM can grant reserves at any time it makes sense – for instance, when a group’s patron gives them extra supplies for a mission – but players can also ask for reserves by making downtime actions.

Here are some examples of reserves in play:

**Suguru Geto**
During downtime, Suguru Geto speaks with some powerful members of the Zenin Clan, one of the Big 3. He knows that his next mission may involve some Zenin assets and territory, so he makes some checks and promises some favors to the Zenin Clan. In exchange, the GM grants him some reserves:
- Cursed Tools: the Zenin Clan gives him access to a Cursed Tool from their vault specifically for this mission
- Backing: the Zenin Clan gives Geto support. If someone messes with him or he starts looking for a promotion, the Zenin Clan will put in a good word for him
- Information: the Zenin Clan has eyes and ears spread throughout the area. Geto can draw on Zenin informants to ask the GM what kinds of curses/curse users he might encounter on this mission.

Typically speaking, reserves are temporary. After they are used (or if they go unused on the mission they were gained for), they are gone. 

Not all downtime activities have to give reserves as a reward. Taking your downtime to train for example, wouldn't grant you temporary reserves, but rather a permanent investment into your skills as a sorcerer.
### Downtime Actions
Downtime actions represent specific activities undertaken by sorcerers between missions. Unless the downtime takes place over an especially long period, players usually only get to make one downtime action per downtime.

In addition to the downtime actions provided here, you can also easily create new ones that are more  relevant to your particular game or story. Use these ones as starting points.

# Powe At a Cost
When you seek Power At A Cost, you’re trying to get your hands on something. Name what you want. You can definitely get it, but depending on the outlandishness of the request, the GM chooses one or two of the following Consequences:
- It’s going to take a lot more time than you thought.
- It’s going to be really risky.
- You’ll have to have to give something up or leave something behind (e.g., wealth, resources, allies).
- You’re going to piss off someone or something important and powerful.
- Things are going to go wildly off-plan.
- You’ll need more information to proceed safely.
- It’s going to fall apart soon.
- You’ll need more resources, but you know where to find them.
- You can get something almost right: a lesser version, or less of it.
# Buy Some Time
When you Buy Some Time, you try to stave off a reckoning, extend a window of opportunity, or merely buy some time and breathing room for you and your group. You might be trying to dodge some heat, survive stranded in the wilderness, or cause a distraction so another plan can reach its climax. You can use that distraction or bought time as reserves for the next mission.

Describe your plan and roll: 
- On 5 or less, you can only buy a little time, and only if drastic measures are taken right now. Otherwise, whatever you’re trying to stave off catches up to you. 
- On a 6-9, you buy enough time, but the situation becomes precarious or desperate. Next time you get this result for the same situation, treat it as 5 or less. 
- On 10+, you buy as much time as you need, until the next downtime session. Next time you get this result for the same situation, treat it as 6-9.
# Gather Information
When you Gather Information, you poke your nose around, perhaps where it doesn’t belong, and investigate something – conducting research, following up on a mystery, tracking a target, or keeping an eye on something. You might head to a library or go undercover to learn what you can. 

Whatever it involves, you’re trying to Gather Information on a subject of your choice. You can use information gained as reserves.

Name your subject and method, and roll:
- On 5 or less, choose one:
	- You get what you’re looking for, but it gets you into trouble straight away.
	- You get out now and avoid trouble.
- On 6–9, you find what you’re looking for, but choose one:
	- You leave clear evidence of your rummaging.
	- You have to dispatch someone or implicate someone innocent to avoid attention.
- On 10+, you get what you’re looking for with no complications.
# Get Into Trouble
When you Get Into Trouble, you blow off some steam, carouse, and generally try to have some fun. You might be trying to make connections, collect gossip, forge a reputation, or even just to forget what happened on the last mission. There’s usually trouble. 

State your intention and roll:
- On 5 or less, decide whether you had good time or not; either way, you wake up the next day somewhere with only one remaining:
	- Your dignity.
	- All of your possessions.
	- Your memory.
- On 6–9, gain one as reserves and lose one:
	- A good reputation.
	- A friend or connection.
	- A useful item or piece of information.\
	- A convenient opportunity.
- On 10+, gain two from the 6–9 list as reserves and don’t lose anything.
# Get Creative
When you Get Creative, you try to use your knowledge of Jujutsu to tweak something or try to make something new – either a Cursed Tool, or some other talisman. Once finished, you can use your new creation as reserves.

Describe your project and roll:
- On 5 or less, you don’t make any progress on your project. Next time you get this result for the same project, treat it as a 6–9.
- On 6–9, you make progress on your project, but don’t quite finish it. You can finish it during your next downtime without rolling, but choose the two things you’re going to need:
	- Quality materials.
	- Specific knowledge or techniques.
	- Specialized tools.
	- A good workspace.
- On 10+, you finish your project before the next mission. If it’s especially complex, treat this as 6–9, but only choose one.
# Get Focused
When you Get Focused, you focus on increasing your own skills for self-improvement. You might practice, learn, meditate, or call on a teacher. 

Name what you want to learn or improve (e.g., a skill, technique, academic subject, or language). The GM will give you a new +1 skill based on your practice and training. For example, the skill could be +1 Playing Chess or +1 Dancing. You can also improve a skill from +1 to +2  or +2 to +3 by taking this downtime action.
# Get Organized
When you Get Focused, you start, run, or improve an organization, business, or other venture. State your organization’s purpose or goal, and choose a Focus: combat, academic, criminal, humanitarian, industrial, entertainment, or political. It begins with +1 in either Efficiency or Influence and +0 in the other, with a maximum of +3.

Efficiency determines how effectively your organization conducts activities within its scope (e.g., a combat organization with high Efficiency would be good at combat). Influence is its size, reach, wealth, and reputation. When your organization directly assists with an activity, you may add either its Efficiency or Influence as a statistic bonus to your skill check. Efficiency is used when performing activities related to your organization’s Focus. Influence is used when acquiring assets, creating opportunities, or swaying public opinion. Advantages gained with the help of your organization can be used as reserves.

Each downtime after the first, roll:
- On 5 or less, choose one or your organization folds immediately:
	- Your organization loses 1 Efficiency and 1 Influence, to a minimum of 0. If both are already at 0, you may not choose this.
	- Your organization needs to pay debts, make an aggressive move, or get bailed out. You choose which, and the GM decides what that looks like.
- On 6–9, your organization is stable. It gains +1 Efficiency or Influence, to a maximum of +3.
- On 10+, your organization gains +1 Efficiency and +1 Influence, to a maximum of +3.
# Get Connected
When you Get Connected, you make connections, call in favors, ask for help, or drum up support for a course of action. You can use your contacts’ resources or aid as reserves for the next mission.

Name your contact and roll:
- On 5 or less, your contact will help you, but you’ve got to do a favor or make good on a promise right now. If you don’t, they won’t help you.
- On 6–9, your contact will help you, but you’ve got to do a favor or make good on a promise afterwards. If you don’t follow through, treat this result as 5 or less next time you get it for the same organization.
- On 10+, your contact will help you, no strings attached. Treat this result as 6–9 next time you get it for the same organization.
# Scrounge and Barter
When you Scrounge and Barter, you try to get your hands on some gear or an asset by dredging the Jujutsu Underworld, chasing down rumors, bartering in secret markets, or hunting around.

You might want some better gear, a Cursed Tool, or other sundries. It needs to be something physical. If you get it, you can take it on the next mission as reserves.

Name what you want and roll:
- On 5 or less, you get what you want, but choose one:
	- It was stolen, probably from someone who’s looking for it.
	- It’s degraded, old, filthy, or damaged.
	- Someone else has it right now and won’t give it up without force or convincing.
- On 6–9, you get what you want, but choose the price you need to pay:
	- Time.
	- Dignity.
	- Reputation.
	- Health, comfort, and wellness.
- On 10+, you get what you’re looking for, no problem.
